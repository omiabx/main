### Goodbye, Krabby Patty? (S9, Feb 20, 2017) [V-T][V-2]

### The real-world study [V-2]
- Kim, Campbell, Shepherd & Kay (2020), *"Understanding contemporary forms of exploitation: Attributions of passion serve to legitimize the poor treatment of workers,"* **Journal of Personality and Social Psychology**, 118(1), 121–148. DOI 10.1037/pspi0000190.
- Across 7 studies and a meta-analysis, people judged unpaid extra hours and demeaning off-job tasks as **more legitimate when the worker was seen as passionate**. They found two mechanisms: (a) the belief that a passionate worker **would have volunteered anyway**, and (b) the belief that for them **the work is its own reward**. The effect was strongest in people high in belief in a just world.
- This lines up almost word for word with SpongeBob: (a) *"I'm workin' all day for free! My treat!"* and (b) *"I can't accept your money... Grilling is my passion."*

### Price tags Krabs has put on SpongeBob (all [V-T])
62 cents (Born Again Krabs) · a nickel (You're Fired) · $50, paid **to** Krabs by Plankton (Chum Bucket) · and SpongeBob's own valuation of his yearly pay: less than a dime (Bummer Vacation).

## 4. Angle options

**A. "The Passion Tax" (recommended).** Every time the door opened, SpongeBob walked back in. He was traded, sold, fired, forced onto vacation and passed over, and each time he came back. Krabs has worked out exactly what that's worth, which is why the price tags are 62 cents, a nickel and "work for me forever." The dark twist is that a 2020 psych study describes this exact arrangement, and the show answered the title question back in 2001: in *Squid on Strike* the answer is two skeletons still on shift. Built for retention, with a clear promise ("the show already answered this, and the answer is grim") and a payoff at the end.

**B. "The Exit Interview."** A case file of every time SpongeBob left the Krusty Krab, run on the reference's notebook scoreboard (LEFT vs CAME BACK) and closing on a verdict. It's lighter and more episode-guide in tone, and it reuses the reference's strongest device. Not as dark.

**C. "Bikini Bottom Labor History."** Map each episode to real U.S. labor history: the strike, the union fine, the unpaid intern, working off a debt. This leans heavily on the archival newsreels. It's the most original, but it drifts away from SpongeBob lore and depends on the archival footage answer.

My recommendation is **A, using B's scoreboard as the running graphic and a short dose of C** (one archival beat, around the study).
