===== CHUNK 1 · COLD OPEN  (226 words) =====

On January twenty-first, two thousand two, Mr. Krabs lost SpongeBob in a card game.

He'd been playing cards with Plankton every Thursday night for fifteen years and he never lost, so one night he got comfortable and put his fry cook in the pot. Plankton had been losing on purpose that entire time, waiting for exactly this.

So SpongeBob goes to work at the Chum Bucket, and Plankton tries everything. He builds him an exact replica of the Krusty Krab kitchen. He gets him fancy new shoes, a bubble bath with an ice cream cone, a toy seahorse to ride on and a bedtime story. At one point, according to Plankton, he even rubs his feet. SpongeBob still won't make a single patty.

Eventually Plankton walks across the street and begs Krabs to take him back, and Krabs has one condition.

Plankton pays it. So Krabs gambled away his best employee and then got paid fifty dollars to take him back. SpongeBob walks into the Krusty Krab with a bandage on his head, and the first thing he does is make Krabs an offer.

So why doesn't he just quit? I went back through every episode where SpongeBob loses that job, and the strange part is that the show already answered the question, about twenty-five years ago. The answer ends with two skeletons. We'll get there.

===== CHUNK 2 · PART 1: MAY I TAKE YOUR ORDER?  (105 words) =====

SpongeBob didn't fall into this job by accident. In the very first episode, which aired May first, nineteen ninety-nine, he's standing outside the Krusty Krab staring at a Help Wanted sign like somebody just proposed to him.

Then he almost talks himself out of going in, because he's convinced he isn't good enough. Patrick has to give him a pep talk, and the first piece of evidence Patrick brings up is from when SpongeBob was a baby.

So the show tells you in episode one that before SpongeBob could say "Mom," he could take your order. Hang on to that, because Mr. Krabs clearly did.

===== CHUNK 3 · PART 2: THE PAYCHECK  (297 words) =====

So what does the most devoted fry cook in the ocean actually get paid? The show never gives a number, but it drops a lot of hints, and none of them are good.

In Squid on Strike, it's payday. Squidward sniffs his envelope like it's a fine wine. Then Krabs holds out SpongeBob's envelope, and SpongeBob turns it down.

Krabs takes the envelope back without saying a word. Then he announces that from now on, he's charging them every time they goof off. Squidward gets an itemized bill that charges him a dollar for breathing and two for existing. SpongeBob doesn't argue. He pulls out a wad of cash and pays on the spot.

He paid his boss for the seconds he spent tying his shoe.

Five years later, in Bummer Vacation, Krabs gets out of paying a union fine and holds up the money he saved. It's one coin, and SpongeBob is honestly impressed.

As for raises, there's an official policy. The Krusty Krab Training Video has a whole section about it called "Interfacing With Your Boss," and the title card for that section is printed over an electric chair, with leather straps on the arms and a cord plugged into the wall.

Krabs says no without looking up from his paperwork, and the narrator congratulates SpongeBob on taking it so well.

Then there's the time somebody asked Krabs directly what SpongeBob is worth. In Born Again Krabs, the Flying Dutchman offers him a trade: he can keep SpongeBob, or he can have whatever money the Dutchman has in his pocket. Seconds earlier, SpongeBob had bet his own soul that Krabs would never sell him out. Krabs has one question.

When Squidward asks how he could trade SpongeBob for sixty-two cents, Krabs has exactly one follow-up.

===== CHUNK 5 · SPONGEBOB MAKES THE NEWS  (87 words) =====

That firing episode actually made the news. Before it even aired in the U.S., the New York Post praised SpongeBob for not, quote, "mooching off the social services of Bikini Bottom." Fox and Friends picked up the line and Al Sharpton answered back on MSNBC. For a little while, grown adults were arguing on television about a sponge's position on food stamps. Everyone fought about Patrick's speech. Nobody seemed bothered that the happy ending was SpongeBob running back to a man who fired him over a nickel.

===== CHUNK 6 · PART 4: THE PASSION TAX  (190 words) =====

There's actual research on this. In twenty twenty, four researchers published a paper in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology called "Understanding contemporary forms of exploitation." Over a series of studies, they found that people think it's more acceptable to make a worker put in unpaid overtime, or do degrading jobs that aren't part of their role, as long as that worker seems passionate about what they do.

They also worked out how people justify it. The first excuse is that a passionate worker would have volunteered anyway. The second is that for someone who loves the job, the work is its own reward.

Now listen to SpongeBob.

SpongeBob makes both excuses himself before Krabs can.

The same researchers found it runs the other way too. When people see a worker being taken advantage of, they're more likely to assume that worker must really love the job.

And Krabs has always known what he's got. When SpongeBob walked out in Nature Pants, Krabs didn't offer him a raise or a day off. He looked at the hat on the ground and said "he'll be back," and he was right.
